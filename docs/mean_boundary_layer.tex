This document specifies how \texttt{pyMack} computes the steady, laminar, compressible flat-plate
boundary layer whose profiles $\bar U$, $\bar T$, $\bar\rho$, $\bar\mu$ and their wall-normal
derivatives are the coefficients of the linear stability problem. Every equation, boundary condition,
coefficient, and default value is stated exactly as implemented.

\paragraph{Notation.} Overbars denote mean (base-flow) quantities. A prime on a mean quantity denotes
$\D\equiv\mathrm d/\mathrm dy$, or $\mathrm d/\mathrm d\eta$ in the similarity coordinate, as stated in
context. The table below collects the base-flow, transport, and scale symbols used here.

\section{Governing equations and non-dimensionalisation}\label{sec:gov}
%=====================================================================
The flow is governed by the compressible Navier--Stokes equations, expressing conservation of mass,
momentum, and energy for a viscous, heat-conducting perfect gas \cite{white2006}:
\begin{align}
  \frac{\Dmat\rho}{\Dmat t}+\rho\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf u &= 0, &&\text{(mass)}\label{eq:ns-mass}\\
  \rho\frac{\Dmat\mathbf u}{\Dmat t} &= -\nabla p+\nabla\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol\tau, &&\text{(momentum)}\label{eq:ns-mom}\\
  \rho\,c_p\frac{\Dmat T}{\Dmat t} &= \frac{\Dmat p}{\Dmat t}+\nabla\!\cdot(\kappa\nabla T)+\Phi, &&\text{(energy)}\label{eq:ns-energy}\\
  p &= \rho\,R_g\,T, &&\text{(state)}\label{eq:ns-state}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf u=(u,v)$ is the velocity, $\rho$ the density, $T$ the temperature, $p$ the pressure, $\mu$
the viscosity, $\kappa$ the conductivity, $c_p$ the specific heat, $R_g$ the specific gas constant (plain
$R$ is reserved for the Mack Reynolds number, Eq.~\eqref{eq:RF}), and
$\Dmat/\Dmat t=\partial_t+\mathbf u\!\cdot\!\nabla$ the material derivative. The remaining terms are the
Newtonian viscous stress tensor and the dissipation function,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tauphi}
  \boldsymbol\tau=\mu\Big(\nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^{\mathsf T}-\tfrac23(\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf u)\,\mathbf I\Big),
  \qquad \Phi=\boldsymbol\tau\!:\!\nabla\mathbf u .
\end{equation}
Their closed forms are not required in what follows; it suffices that both are linear in the velocity
gradients. Equations \eqref{eq:ns-mass}--\eqref{eq:ns-state} are applied twice: once to obtain the steady
mean flow below, and once in linearised form about that mean flow to obtain the disturbance equations
of linear stability theory.

\paragraph{Non-dimensionalisation and default gas constants.} Velocities are scaled by the edge speed
$U_e$, temperature by $T_e$, density by $\rho_e$, viscosity by $\mu_e$, and pressure by $\rho_e U_e^2$.
Because the gas is perfect and the pressure is nearly uniform across the layer, $\bar\rho=1/\bar T$ in
these units. Three dimensionless groups govern the problem,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:groups}
  \Mae=\frac{U_e}{a_e},\quad a_e=\sqrt{\gamma R_g T_e},\qquad
  \Ree=\frac{\rho_e U_e L_{\mathrm{ref}}}{\mu_e},\qquad
  \mathit{Pr}=\frac{\mu_e c_p}{\kappa_e},
\end{equation}
where $a_e$ is the edge speed of sound. The default gas constants are the standard air values
$\gamma=1.4$, $\mathit{Pr}=0.72$, Sutherland constant $S=110.4~\mathrm K$ \cite{sutherland1893}, and
power-law exponent $\omega_\mu\approx0.7$ when the power-law viscosity model is selected.

\paragraph{The Mack length and Reynolds scales.}\label{sec:Lstar} A flat-plate boundary layer grows as
$\sqrt x$, so the natural local length scale at station $x$, following Mack \cite{mack1984}, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lstar}
  L^*=\sqrt{\frac{\nu_e\,x}{U_e}},\qquad \nu_e=\frac{\mu_e}{\rho_e}.
\end{equation}
With $L_{\mathrm{ref}}=L^*$ the Reynolds number becomes the square root of the conventional one, and a
fixed physical frequency $f$ maps to a reduced frequency $F$; both enter the neutral-curve and $N$-factor
computations:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RF}
  R=\frac{U_eL^*}{\nu_e}=\sqrt{Re_x},\quad Re_x=\frac{U_ex}{\nu_e};\qquad
  F=\frac{2\pi f\,\nu_e}{U_e^2};\qquad \alpha_L=\alpha L^* ,
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the streamwise wavenumber. $R$ is the abscissa of every neutral curve and $\alpha_L$
the ordinate. The solver itself is dimensionless; the conversions above are pure postprocessing.

\section{The self-similar base flow}\label{sec:base}
%=====================================================================
The mean flow is obtained from a boundary-value problem, not an eigenvalue problem: the data are
prescribed at both ends of the domain ($y=0$ and $y\to\infty$) and the solution is unique. Its unknowns
are the steady laminar profiles $\bar U$, $\bar T$, $\bar\rho$, $\bar\mu$ and their wall-normal
derivatives, which subsequently enter the linearised disturbance equations as known coefficients.

For a flat plate the mean flow is \emph{self-similar}: profiles at different streamwise stations collapse
onto a single curve in the stretched coordinate $\eta$. Introducing the dimensionless stream function
$f(\eta)$ with $f'=\bar U=u/U_e$ (in this section a prime denotes $\mathrm d/\mathrm d\eta$), the momentum
and energy equations \eqref{eq:ns-mom}--\eqref{eq:ns-energy} reduce under the boundary-layer approximation
to the classical pair of coupled ordinary differential equations
\cite{white2006,ozgen2008},
\begin{align}
  \big(C\,f''\big)' + f\,f'' &= 0, \label{eq:bl-mom}\\
  \big(B\,\bar T{}'\big)' + f\,\bar T{}' + (\gamma-1)\,\Mae^2\,C\,\big(f''\big)^2 &= 0, \label{eq:bl-energy}
\end{align}
with the transport groups
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transport}
  C=\frac{\mu/\mu_e}{T/T_e},\qquad K=\frac{\kappa}{c_p\mu_e},\qquad
  B=\frac{K}{\bar T}\Big(=\tfrac{C}{\mathit{Pr}}\ \text{at constant }\mathit{Pr}\Big),
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the Chapman--Rubesin parameter \cite{chapman1949}. The final term of \eqref{eq:bl-energy}
represents aerodynamic heating: it elevates the near-wall temperature at high Mach number and couples the
energy equation to the velocity field. The conductivity model is $\kappa=\mu c_p/\mathit{Pr}$, so that
$\bar\kappa=\bar\mu/\mathit{Pr}$ at constant $\mathit{Pr}$; the transport derivatives
$\mathrm d\mu/\mathrm dT$, $\mathrm d\kappa/\mathrm dT$ are evaluated analytically from the selected
viscosity law.

\paragraph{Boundary conditions.} Equations \eqref{eq:bl-mom}--\eqref{eq:bl-energy} are closed by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:blbc}
  \underbrace{f(0)=0,\ f'(0)=0}_{\text{no slip}},\quad
  \underbrace{f'(\infty)=1,\ \bar T(\infty)=1}_{\text{match the edge}},\qquad
  \begin{cases}\bar T{}'(0)=0 &\text{(adiabatic wall)}\\ \bar T(0)=T_w/T_e &\text{(isothermal wall)}\end{cases}
\end{equation}
For an adiabatic wall the recovery temperature is the standard result
$T_{aw}/T_e=1+\tfrac12(\gamma-1)\sqrt{\mathit{Pr}}\,\Mae^2$ \cite{white2006}. For a cone the edge state itself is computed
first, from the Taylor--Maccoll equation behind the conical shock; this is a separate upstream
calculation and is not detailed further here.

\paragraph{Numerical solution.} Equations \eqref{eq:bl-mom}--\eqref{eq:bl-energy} with conditions
\eqref{eq:blbc} are solved as a five-state first-order system $[\,f,\bar U,\bar U',\bar T,\bar T'\,]$ by
fourth-order collocation with residual control and Newton iteration \cite{kierzenka2001,virtanen2020},
integrated to $\eta_{\max}\approx40$, well
beyond the boundary-layer edge. On any requested grid the solver returns
$\bar U,\bar U',\bar U'',\ \bar T,\bar T',\bar T'',\ \bar\rho,\ \bar\mu,\ \mathrm d\mu/\mathrm dT,\
\mathrm d^2\mu/\mathrm dT^2,\ \bar\kappa,\ \mathrm d\kappa/\mathrm dT,\ \mathrm d^2\kappa/\mathrm dT^2$.

Figure~\ref{fig:baseflow} shows the computed profiles for a representative hypersonic case: $\Mae=6$,
edge temperature $T_e=54~\mathrm K$, and an isothermal wall at $T_w=300~\mathrm K$, i.e.\
$T_w/T_e=5.56$. Since the adiabatic recovery temperature at these conditions is
$T_{aw}\approx7.1\,T_e$, the wall is cooled. The velocity rises monotonically to its edge value, while
viscous heating produces an interior temperature maximum, $\bar T\approx5.7\,T_e$, just above the cooled
wall. The dotted line marks the outermost generalised inflection point, $(\bar\rho\,\bar U')'=0$, the
seat of the inviscid instability identified by Lees \& Lin \cite{leeslin1946}.

\paragraph{Remark: two equivalent formulations.} Two equivalent self-similar formulations share the
five-equation structure above. Equations
\eqref{eq:bl-mom}--\eqref{eq:bl-energy} and the $\sqrt2$ transform of Section~\ref{sec:transform}
constitute the classical compressible-Blasius form. The temporal solver uses instead the formulation
of \"Ozgen \& K{\i}rcal{\i} \cite{ozgen2008}, with that reference's transport laws and normalisation
($f'=\bar U/\bar T$, factors of $\tfrac12$, $y/L^*=\eta$, no $\sqrt2$). The two yield the same physical
profiles; only the similarity bookkeeping differs.

\section{Transformation to physical coordinates}\label{sec:transform}
%=====================================================================
The similarity solution is computed once, in $\eta$; all case-specific dimensional information enters
only through the transformation back to the physical wall-normal coordinate. Because the near-wall gas is
hot ($\bar T>1$) and correspondingly rarefied ($\bar\rho=1/\bar T$), equal increments of $\eta$ span
unequal increments of physical distance. The map follows directly from the temperature field. In the
units of Section~\ref{sec:gov} the differential relation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dydeta}
  \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm d\eta}=\sqrt2\,\bar T(\eta)\qquad(y\ \text{in units of}\ L^*),
\end{equation}
whose integral gives the physical coordinate at station $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ytransform}
  \frac{y}{L^*}=\sqrt2\int_0^\eta \bar T(\eta')\,\mathrm d\eta' .
\end{equation}
Substituting the Mack length \eqref{eq:Lstar} yields the fully dimensional, case-specific form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ydim}
  \boxed{\;y^*(\eta;x^*)=\sqrt{\frac{2\,\nu_e\,x^*}{U_e}}\,\int_0^\eta \bar T(\eta')\,\mathrm d\eta'\;}
\end{equation}
so that a single similarity solution generates the entire family of dimensional profiles, parameterised
by the streamwise station $x^*$ and the edge state $(U_e,\nu_e)$. The map \eqref{eq:ytransform} is
strictly monotone, and its inverse assigns a unique $\eta$ to any physical height $y^*$. Since
$\bar T>1$ near the wall, the transform outpaces the linear reference $y/L^*=\sqrt2\,\eta$ there: a
uniform $\eta$ mesh maps to a physical mesh stretched into the hot near-wall region, which is why $\eta$,
rather than $y$, is the natural computational coordinate. Wall-normal derivatives convert by the chain
rule implied by \eqref{eq:dydeta},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chain}
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dy}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2\,\bar T(\eta)}\,\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\eta}
  \qquad(y\ \text{in units of}\ L^*).
\end{equation}

The integral thicknesses follow from the same transform,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thicknesses}
  \frac{\delta^*}{L^*}=\sqrt2\int_0^\infty(\bar T-\bar U)\,\mathrm d\eta,\qquad
  \frac{\theta}{L^*}=\sqrt2\int_0^\infty\bar U\,(1-\bar U)\,\mathrm d\eta,
\end{equation}
where the displacement thickness $\delta^*$ measures the outward displacement of the outer flow by the
layer and the momentum thickness $\theta$ its momentum deficit. A profile may be stored on a $y/\delta^*$
or a $y/L^*$ grid; the constant $\delta^*/L^*$ converts between them, with
$\D\mapsto(\delta^*/L^*)^{-1}\D$.

The profiles $\bar U,\bar T,\bar\rho,\bar\mu$ and their wall-normal derivatives assembled here are
the coefficients of the linearised disturbance equations of linear stability theory.
